<p class="lead">Flexible couplings are built to tolerate some misalignment. That is exactly why misalignment goes unnoticed: the machine keeps running while the bearings, seals and coupling inserts on both sides wear out early.</p>

## Two kinds of misalignment

- **Offset (parallel):** the shaft centerlines are parallel but not on the same line. Measured in mils (thousandths of an inch) or millimetres at the coupling center.
- **Angular:** the centerlines meet at an angle. Expressed as a gap difference per inch (or per 100 mm) of coupling diameter.

Real machines almost always have both, in both the vertical and the horizontal plane. That makes four values to correct.

## How close is close enough

Tolerances depend mainly on speed: the faster the machine, the tighter the numbers. Typical published values for short couplings look like this:

| Speed (RPM) | Offset (mils) | Angularity (mils/in) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1,200 | ±2.5 | 0.5 |
| 1,800 | ±2.0 | 0.3 |
| 3,600 | ±1.0 | 0.2 |

## Fix soft foot before anything else

If one foot of the motor doesn't sit flat, tightening its bolt bends the frame, and your readings change every time you touch the bolts. Check each foot with a dial indicator while loosening it. More than about 2 mils of movement needs shims to correct it.

<Note title="On the floor">
Use no more than three or four shims per foot, and use clean pre-cut stainless shims. A stack of rusty, bent shims behaves like a spring.
</Note>

## Thermal growth

A machine aligned cold may be out of alignment at operating temperature, because the hot side grows. Pumps on hot fluids and gearboxes are classic examples. The manufacturer often gives a cold offset target so the shafts line up when hot.
